## 1. Three kinds of reading with a clear job

| Material | Agreed treatment at the shown level | Why the child encounters it |
|---|---|---|
| Instructions and goal/action text | Short, direct wording naming the object/action and any necessary condition; D030's full crossing instruction stays available while relevant | Work out and carry out an action, with the condition available for rereading |
| Character conversations | Natural exchanges with clear speaker attribution; keep the relevant conversation available while the child chooses a reply | Understand a person's situation, make a proposal, communicate, and react to the reply |
| Story pages | A complete short account with a title, author, ordinary paragraphs, and enough context to follow the people/events; show a readable page rather than requiring a click for each sentence | Follow what happened and use it to shape an illustration, ending, discussion, or narration |

These are content/presentation directions, not final panel dimensions or scroll/input contracts. Keep readable spacing and preserve ordinary paragraph/dialogue structure. Scrolling or suitable page access must accommodate text size without cutting off a passage. Full layout/focus/access remains R15-R16. Do not force every story to an identical word count or inflate Sol's intentionally short unfinished manuscript.

Write natural English for the agreed ages 9-12. Name people and actions clearly; preserve who is speaking, when events happened, and what is an interpretation. Include useful challenging words in meaningful situations. Different passages can offer different amounts of challenge. No formal grade-level score, minimum reading ability, or learning outcome is asserted from this editorial direction.

## 2. Agreed complete Mara page

Use the exact page below, accepted in this dedicated R09 review. Display the title and author:

**The Torn Wing — by Mara**

> Everyone had stepped off the last boat except a boy holding a paper bird.
>
> "Are you waiting for someone?" I asked.
>
> He shook his head.
>
> "This is for my grandmother. My sister made it, but she's too ill to come. I promised my sister I'd keep it safe."
>
> One wing was torn.
>
> I reached toward the bird, but he pulled it closer.
>
> "Would you like some help?" I asked.
>
> He hesitated. Then he held out the bird.
>
> I fetched some tape from the dock office. Together, we mended the wing.
>
> When he stepped onto the dock, he held the bird carefully in both hands.
>
> "Now I can tell my grandmother how we fixed it," he said.

Mara is the first-person narrator. The boy's sister and grandmother belong to her remembered account; the grandmother is not automatically Pip's Grandma. The page appears only through the actual applicable story interaction/source availability. Its remembered boat arrival cannot change the current gathering clock or passenger state.

This page offers a promise, a damaged gift, initial reluctance, an offer of help, and a repair made together. Those details make the two already-agreed lantern illustrations meaningful. The text leaves room for the child to consider why the boy pulled the bird closer; it does not supply a new mandatory inference question or a claim that choosing an illustration proves understanding.
