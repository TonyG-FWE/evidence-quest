## Voice and story direction

Pip asks concrete questions and notices what people do. Mara pays attention to small gestures without claiming to know a stranger’s thoughts. Sol is precise, skilled and occasionally dry; his doubt concerns sharing his ordinary work. Rina has bread to make and people waiting for it. Grandma is affectionate and sometimes funny, but she has allowed disappointment to become an untested assumption.

The friendship’s difficulties predate the storm. The bridge repair restores access; listening, invitations and the gathering address the reasons people stayed away. All valid contributions remain equal. Neither a finished manuscript nor Mara’s attendance is the preferred moral outcome. Older stories show the friendship’s texture and remain optional.

## Pip’s visit and encounters

### A promise to Grandma

*Pip's adventure*

On his last visit, Pip had held out his palm while Grandma tipped a lantern seed into it. It was smaller than he had expected. He could close his hand around it without making a fist.

“Keep it in your backpack,” she had said. “Next time, we’ll plant it together. There’s a place for it beside the others.” Pip had promised to come before dark today. All the way to the river, he had been imagining where his flower might stand among hers.

Now the footbridge was gone. Last night’s storm had left a gap between the banks. Pip could see the garden on the other side, but seeing it was not the same as reaching it. He checked the seed, then unfolded Grandma’s letter. She had written it before the storm.

“The garden is quiet these days. Mara and Sol hardly visit anymore. I think they don’t enjoy coming now. Yesterday I thought I heard someone on the path, but it was only the leaves. I miss their stories. I’m glad you and I have a seed to plant.”

### Mara's story can travel

*At the passenger dock*

Mara stood at the passenger dock with a sheet of paper in her hand. She had folded it carefully enough to keep the writing on the inside.

“Is that a story?” Pip asked.

“One I wrote for Grandma’s garden. I keep bringing it to work with me.”

“Grandma says you hardly visit. She thinks you don’t enjoy going there anymore.”

Mara lowered the page. “I miss it. My work hours changed before the storm. I used to finish before the storytelling began. Now I wait for the last boat and help everyone ashore. By the time I could reach the garden, they would have gone home. This page has made more trips to the dock than to Grandma.”

“Could you leave a little earlier?”

“I have an obligation to the passengers. Someone may need a hand with that last step. I can’t go while they’re still on the boat.” She looked across the river. “But I would like Grandma to hear my story.”

### What Pip found at Rina’s bakery

*Pip’s visit today*

Rain dripped through a cracked roof tile at Rina’s bakery. Between drips, Pip could hear the scrape of a flour sack being dragged across the floor. Rina stopped to catch her breath, then pulled it farther from the puddle.

“I promised to have everyone’s bread ready today,” she said. “The people who ordered it will come whether my roof is ready or not. Help me keep this flour dry, and I can still get the dough started.”

Sol had brought his tools and a ladder. He was looking up at the roof. An intact spare tile rested on a shelf beside the bakery.

### A story reaches Grandma

*Pip and Grandma*

## The stories in the garden

### The Torn Wing

*by Mara*

Everyone had stepped off the last boat except a boy holding a paper bird. The operator waited while I went back to see whether he needed a hand. He stood very still, though the boat moved gently against the dock.

“Are you waiting for someone?” I asked. He shook his head. One wing of the bird was torn. Its tip hung lower than the other, and his thumb rested just below the tear.

“This is for my grandmother,” he said. “My sister made it, but she’s too ill to come. I promised my sister I’d keep it safe.” He turned it a little, as if there might be an angle from which the wing still looked whole.

I reached toward the bird. He pulled it closer. My hand stopped between us. For a moment, neither of us said anything. Water tapped against the side of the boat.

“Would you like some help?” I asked. “We could look at it together.” He hesitated. Then he held out the bird, with both hands underneath it.

I fetched some tape from the dock office. When I came back, he was holding the torn edges near each other. “Like this?” he asked. I bent down so I could see. “That looks right. Keep them there while I lay the tape.”

Together, we mended the wing. He held the paper steady while I laid the tape across the tear. We smoothed it gently. The folds his sister had made were still there. So was a small shiny line where we had worked.

He raised the bird and looked underneath. Then he turned it toward me. “You can see where we fixed it.” “Yes,” I said.

When he stepped onto the dock, he carried the bird carefully in both hands. At the last step he paused, then found a clear place for his foot.

“Now I can tell my grandmother how we fixed it,” he said. He tilted the bird once, very slightly, and the mended wing caught the light.

### A Small Repair

*by Sol · Ending not written yet*

Rain dripped through the roof of Rina’s bakery. I could hear it before I reached the door: a steady tap, a pause, another tap. Rina was dragging her flour sacks away from the puddles. She had moved one, but the water was spreading toward the next.

“I promised to have everyone’s bread ready today,” she said. “If those sacks get wet, I won’t be able to bake.” She looked from the flour to the roof, then back to the flour. I set down my tools.

From the ladder I found the cracked tile. The crack was narrow, but rain had a way through it. Rina had an intact spare. With her permission, Pip brought it from the shelf and carried it over to me. He held it with both hands until I had a firm grip.

I lifted out the damaged tile and set it aside. Then I fitted the spare into the opening. I eased it into place and checked the edges. From below, Rina asked whether it would hold. I gave it one last careful look before climbing down.

We listened. Rain still pattered on the roof outside. Inside, the dripping had stopped. Rina bent over the sacks and checked the flour. It was still dry. She let her shoulders drop and stood up.

I looked once more at the ceiling. There was nothing moving there now, so there was nothing more for me to fix. I packed my tools, made sure I had left the damaged tile out of the way, and went back to my workshop.

### The Empty Bench

*by Grandma*

Before the storm, I put cushions on the garden bench every evening. I knew which corner of the bench stayed warm longest. I knew where to sit so I could see the path and still leave room for someone beside me.

Mara brought stories about people she met at the dock. Sometimes she began laughing before she reached the funny part. Sol told us about things he had made. He would hold his hands apart to show the size of something, and we would have to move our cups.

Then their visits became less frequent. At first, I set out the cushions as usual. If I heard leaves scrape along the path, I looked up. Sometimes I had already begun to smile before I saw that nobody was there.

“Perhaps they don’t enjoy our stories anymore,” I thought. I could have gone to ask. Instead, I sat a little longer. Eventually I put the spare cushions away. The bench was easier to clear, but I did not like the look of it.

All that happened before the storm took the footbridge. By then, the garden had already grown quiet.

Today Pip brought me Mara’s explanation. Her work hours had changed. While I was waiting here, she was waiting for the last passengers to come ashore. Then I asked Sol what had kept him away. He thought no one would want to hear about the ordinary things he made and fixed.

I had assumed my friends no longer cared about the garden. Yet Mara had written a story for me, and Sol had brought his page. I had been looking at the empty places beside me and guessing what they meant.

Pip helped us find a way to share our stories again. Before I finished this page, I opened the storage beside the bench and brought out the cushions. I set them where people could reach them.

One corner had folded under. I pulled it straight, then sat down with my story.

### The Windy Picnic

*by Grandma · an older garden story*

I was spreading a cloth over the garden table when the wind snatched one corner from my hand. I still held the opposite corner, which meant I had a very poor kite and nowhere sensible to put it.

“Hold on,” I said, mostly to the cloth. It flapped across the table toward a jug of water. Sol caught it before it knocked the jug over. For a moment we stood with our arms stretched out, looking at each other over the table.

“We’ll need to secure the corners,” he said. “Unless you want us to eat standing like this.”

Mara fetched four smooth stones from beside the path. We placed one on each corner. I eased my fingers away first. Sol let go next. The cloth lifted a little between the stones, then settled.

We set out our picnic. The wind ruffled our hair and rattled the leaves, but the cloth stayed put. I could finally use both hands to cut the cake.

“Next time, I’ll bring a heavier tablecloth,” I said. Sol considered this while he finished his slice.

“Bring the same cake,” he said.

Mara held out her plate for another slice. I cut her a piece before the wind could suggest any more improvements.

### The Unexpected Duet

*by Mara · an older garden story*

A boy carried a flute onto my boat. He kept it tucked beneath his arm while the other passengers found their seats. Whenever someone passed, he turned a little to keep it out of the way.

“Would you like to play something?” I asked. He looked down at the flute, then along the boat. “You don’t have to,” I added.

He hesitated, then lifted it. His fingers found their places. The first notes were so quiet that I could hardly hear them over the water. I stayed where I was and listened.

A whistle answered from the back of the boat. An older passenger had recognised the tune. The boy stopped. The whistle stopped too. They looked at one another across the seated passengers.

The boy started again. This time, they played together. When he drew breath, the whistle carried on for a note, and he came back in beside it. A passenger who had been talking lowered her voice.

As we reached the dock, the tune was nearly finished. The boy could see people gathering their things, but he kept playing. The other passengers waited for the final note before stepping ashore.

I let them pass. The boy lowered his flute and looked toward the back of the boat once more.
