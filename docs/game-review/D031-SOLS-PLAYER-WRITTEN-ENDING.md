## The new participation

At Sol's workshop, let the child write a short ending in their own words and see that ending performed by paper characters in a small story scene. They can revise the wording and compare what the scene emphasizes before asking Sol to use it at the garden. The more demanding task is expressing why a small repair mattered, using the account and deciding which consequence to emphasize.

The agreed presentation is that Sol's page opens into a small paper scene on his workbench. It depicts his past experience; Pip and Sol remain beside it at the workshop. Rina is a character in that depiction; the bakery is not a new present-day destination or a roof-repair quest. ## Exact agreed prompt and successful example

Sol asks:

> What did the repair make possible? Write an ending that shows why it mattered.

The child can write one or two sentences. For example, continuing Sol's first-person account:

> I thought I had only fixed a roof tile. But keeping the flour dry helped Rina bake the bread she had promised.

They choose **Try my ending**. The small story scene shows the repaired roof, dry flour, and Rina using it to bake. Rain can continue outside; time progresses through a short story transition rather than implying instantaneous baking. The child's exact draft stays visible next to this rehearsal. The existing source account remains available for comparison.

For this supported emphasis, Sol can give D016's existing response:

> That explains why the repair mattered. I'll use that ending.

For this proposed interaction, that acceptance is presented when the child chooses **Use this ending**, rather than replacing their page merely because they tried a rehearsal. Trying, revising, Sol's acceptance, preparing his page, and the later actual sharing remain separate events. Final control labels and complete state conditions require later review.

The player may instead write:

That rehearsal emphasizes Rina's later visit to Sol's workshop. The corresponding existing acceptance is:

Their choice therefore changes the written ending and its performed focus. The shown third staging pattern can show both confirmed consequences in order: Rina bakes, then later thanks Sol. ## A misunderstanding and a useful response

Suppose the child writes:

> I fixed the roof, so the rain stopped.

The shown and agreed response is:

> The repair stopped water getting into the bakery. It didn't stop the rain outside. Look again at what Rina needed to keep dry.

Keep the child's draft and the relevant original sentences available. They can revise and try again, use an authored ending, or have Sol share his original draft. A draft with unresolved facts can remain the child's draft; it is not silently adopted as Sol's factual account or presented as something he witnessed. There is no comprehension score or requirement to correct an answer before continuing the adventure through an already valid contribution route.

The rehearsal never depicts the suggested weather change as a true event. Source uncertainty or an ambiguous draft needs a short clarification or a clearly limited preview; do not silently substitute another ending or pretend the text was understood. Exact feedback across possible writing remains R09/R13/R14 work. No mistaken sentence is mandatory.
