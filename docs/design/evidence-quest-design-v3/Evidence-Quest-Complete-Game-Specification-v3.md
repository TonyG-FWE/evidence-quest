## 1. The adventure, from beginning to end

You belong to SparkFest’s story crew. Your group has made a tiny paper traveler named Pip; you made Pip’s little backpack. Today, your small creation will become a huge moving shadow on the festival stage. You are the premiere captain: bring the crew’s work together and start its first public show.

The story is called **The Little Bridge**. Pip promised Grandma that they would plant a special seed together on her hill before dark. But last night’s rain washed away the footbridge. Pip and the seed are on one bank, Grandma is on the other, and the friends nearby have only tiny paper boats. The unfinished story stops there. The player’s task is to help make the rest of it happen.

At the opening, the child can immediately pull a large tab on the crew’s paper rehearsal model. Pip moves toward the river; the broken bridge folds down. Jo, the writer and stage coordinator, explains: “You made Pip’s backpack. Today, you’re running our premiere. Let’s get your little traveler onto the big stage.” There is a visible player character, a physical interaction, a recognizable problem, and a reason the finished show belongs to this crew.

Then the player tries the stage’s Preview control. The main backdrop remains empty. Loop, the rolling projector robot, is missing from its dock.

Jo is concerned about the unfinished show, but not baffled about the departure. She lent Loop to Ari to capture the final lantern-flower animation. She let Ari choose a suitable recording space and did not follow the move. “Ari borrowed Loop to finish the flower shot. They were finding somewhere the paper would stay still. I haven’t seen them return.”

Remy, who built the paper boats, has a worrying interpretation. Their short clip shows Loop passing through the crew door, and their photo of the courtyard notice shows the word **CANCELED**. “I think our whole premiere is off.”

The child can pursue that claim in the courtyard, investigate the indoor recording spaces, inspect the crew’s request, or talk to the makers. No selected theory locks the other routes.

Following the cancellation lead produces an actual discovery. The player crosses the courtyard, releases the bottom clip of a curled notice, and pulls it flat. Its heading is **OUTDOOR REHEARSAL**. The rest explains that wind keeps folding the paper petals; the opening premiere is still planned, and the flower shot is being finished indoors. The player has discovered which event was canceled. The photograph was real, but incomplete. The child can show Remy the full notice, and Remy can revise the claim without becoming a villain or the target of a joke.

An independent clue also supports investigating indoors: Ari’s recording request says the flower needs a still space and a plain wall. The Media Room has both. The workshop has moving scenery and occupied work surfaces; the courtyard is windy. These observations make Media a sensible place to check, not a magically proven current location.

In Media, Loop is physically present, sound and ready to return. Ari has completed the flower animation. Ari immediately explains the move and offers the robot, the finished animation, the four reusable story tiles, and both creators’ notes. Nothing is hidden behind a question. If the child comes here first, that works.

The revelation has two parts: **the premiere was never abandoned, and the apparently small contributions are enough to make its ending work.** Loop’s move was part of finishing the story. Finding the robot resolves the location question; the player’s original premiere assignment is still unfinished.

Back onstage, the child places physical story tiles into a short rehearsal rail. Each tile tells Loop to animate a particular event in the paper world: send the seed across in one boat; join the boats and help Pip cross; plant the seed with Grandma; or grow its light. These are reusable puppet cues, not numbered film frames with one prescribed order.

Jo’s note says Pip promised to go to Grandma and plant the seed **with her**. Remy’s note explains that one boat can carry the seed but not Pip, while connected boats make a crossing. Neither note alone provides the whole plan.

The child may try sending the seed first. The boat sails across successfully. Grandma catches it. But Pip is still on the opposite bank. If the next tile asks them to plant together, Grandma waits with the seed and Pip looks across the river. The story has not achieved what Pip promised. No red grade is necessary: the rehearsal makes the missing part visible.

The player can return to the notes, ask for help, and move the tiles. Joining the boats lets Pip cross. If the seed has already arrived, Pip collects it from Grandma; otherwise Pip carries it across. They plant together. Once the seed is in the hill’s soil, it grows into a lantern-flower that lights both banks.

The player can use the direct plan or include a harmless seed-ferry step. Both are accepted. The objective is to make the creators’ story work, not to guess the designer’s shortest sequence.

After a successful rehearsal, the child presses the same stage control whose opening preview failed. The big backdrop becomes the river, the boats form their bridge, Pip’s backpack is recognizable, and the lantern-flower spreads warm light across the stage. Jo and Remy’s separate contributions become one story. Loop turns toward the player, and the crew celebrates the premiere they completed together.

There is no confession, secret thief, repair diagnosis, or final essay. The mystery concerns the meaning of incomplete information; its resolution leads directly into finishing the show that caused Loop to be borrowed.

### Why a child might want to play

The proposed appeal is ownership of a small character’s big premiere; the uncertainty about whether the show is still happening; finding the information another character missed; physically opening and operating things; making an animated world respond to a plan; and seeing a modest paper creation fill the stage. The rehearsal includes small expressive reactions and permits experimentation. Maximum Toast provides one optional absurd surprise during travel.

These are design hypotheses. Their appeal must be observed in play. The cancellation misunderstanding is deliberately a short opening mystery, not a claim to sustain twenty minutes by itself. The substantial second half is part of the same premiere assignment from the opening.

### The obvious-question audit

**Why can’t Jo just name the room?** Ari was authorized to choose a suitable space; Jo remained onstage. She says what she knows and identifies Ari as the person who can confirm it.

**Why doesn’t Ari immediately end the mystery?** Ari does when encountered: the location and filming facts are supplied without a gate. Reaching Ari is a valid short route. The same premiere assignment then continues into the story rehearsal.

**Why are there multiple tiles rather than a completed movie?** The crew built a reusable paper-puppet story player. The tiles trigger actions in that world; the final flower animation was the last missing asset. The player was assigned final rehearsal and launch, not asked to reconstruct a randomly corrupted file.

**Why doesn’t a writer simply dictate the answer?** The child has a real crew role and can attempt it. Both makers offer their full notes, collaborate when shown evidence, and give a direct solution if asked. The game records that support rather than withholding it or claiming independent reading.

**Is the cancellation reveal enough of a mystery?** It is enough for an opening investigation with an optional detour. The complete case must earn its interest through the connected puppet rehearsal and premiere. If later play finds that combination uninteresting, revise the narrative rather than padding the cancellation search.

## 4. Educational design and what counts as evidence

### Primary skill

**Use relevant details from more than one source to infer a reason or plan that the sources do not state completely, and revise it when a detail conflicts.**

In this case a child must read beyond a prominent word, combine a purpose with conditions, and connect one creator’s story goal with another creator’s description of what the props can do.

This is a plausible target around grades 4–6. Official reading standards progress through supporting inferences with textual details and explaining how events and character responses develop a story. That is a design rationale, not certification, calibrated difficulty, or proof of learning. [Grade 4 reading standards](https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/RL/4/), [Grade 5 reading standards](https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/RL/5/), [Grade 6 reading standards](https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/RL/6/).

| Use | Information to connect | Child’s possible inference | Action and revealing consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Choose a search plan** | Ari’s requirements in E4 plus the discovered navigation text NAV.MEDIA describing an indoor filming space with a plain wall | “Media fits what Ari needs, so that is a sensible place to check.” | Choose Media or ask a source-specific question. Ari and direct observation confirm the actual move. Suitability does not prove occupancy; E3 is optional. |
| **Make the intended story happen** | Jo’s promise in E6 requires Pip’s personal arrival and joint planting; Remy’s E7 says one boat carries only the seed and linked boats provide a crossing | “Sending the seed alone does not let Pip keep the promise. Pip also needs a way across.” | Add the bridge action, rehearse, see Pip join Grandma and plant. The seed-ferry action remains valid as an extra step. |
| **Apply an explicit condition within that story** | Remy’s E7 describes the hill-soil condition for growth | “The light can’t help them cross first: it needs the planting to happen.” | Run a plan that plants before growing light. A premature light cue leaves the seed unlit. This is supporting comprehension of a stated condition, not another two-source inference or an independent post-test. |

Supporting skills are reading scope words such as “outdoor” and “still”; distinguishing a recorded observation from “I think”; and understanding a character’s promise and a prerequisite. They serve the primary skill rather than becoming separate scored curricula.

### Why the writing earns its place

The full notice tells which event the prominent cancellation word modifies. The filming request supplies a purpose for the movement. The visual boat can genuinely carry the seed, so it is not automatically an obviously bad prop. Jo’s wording establishes why seed delivery alone is insufficient. Remy’s note supplies a way to satisfy that missing goal. No numbered recipe supplies the complete rehearsal.

The physical rehearsal can also be solved by experimentation. The game should welcome that. It cannot claim that correct actions alone demonstrate reading comprehension. The design therefore creates two voluntary opportunities to capture reasoning before a revealing result, while keeping free action available.

### Two natural reasoning opportunities

**A — Crew lead:** after viewing Ari’s request and the venue description, a small “Tell crew my plan” action lets the child select up to two short source passages and add one sentence. Example: “Ari needs still paper and a plain wall. Media is an indoor filming space with a plain wall, so I’m checking there.” E4 and NAV.MEDIA can be selected. Travel remains available without doing this. Correcting Remy from E3 is recorded separately as scope comprehension or revision.

**B — Rehearsal plan:** before pressing Rehearse, “Explain this plan to Jo” allows the same lightweight source selection and sentence. Example: “Pip has to plant with Grandma, and one boat only carries the seed. The joined boats get Pip there too.” It does not unlock the rail or the show.

The two-source requirement describes the evidence needed to support the educational claim; it is not a button-permission requirement. A valid spontaneous explanation can count even if the child identifies the sources verbally in their typed sentence instead of using both source slots. Ambiguity gets a clarification, not a failure label.

### Honest observation categories

| Recorded behavior | Permitted interpretation |
|---|---|
| Relevant details from both sources plus a defensible connection, before a source-specific or explicit solution hint | Observed independent inference in this situation; model interpretation remains reviewable and fallible |
| A valid connection after content help | Supported performance, with the actual assistance recorded |
| Child identifies a contradictory detail and changes the claim | Observed evidence-based revision, with assistance level |
| Repeated experiments without an explanation | Experimentation observed; reading reasoning unobserved |
| Correct route or successful show without a rationale | Game success; reading reasoning unobserved |
| Child says they guessed | Self-reported guess, not a label inferred from click patterns |
| Long pause, repeated walking, or replaying the joke | No inference about ability, attention, or comprehension |

Record source discovery, explanation, selected passages, action prediction, actual action/result, and assistance in order. An explanation after the result is reflection, not retroactive independent prediction. Access support such as enlarged text is separate from answer help. Read-aloud can support understanding but cannot demonstrate independent printed-word decoding.

The second context can show a child applying an approach again during the case. It is not evidence of long-term improvement. Do not promise reading fluency or vocabulary gains that this build has not specifically taught or measured.

### Four connected areas

| Area | Reason to visit and principal objects | Exits and changed use |
|---|---|---|
| **Main Stage — hub** | Jo; Pip’s pull-tab model; Loop’s empty dock; E1 crew brief and E4 request; E6 note; story-tile rail and Preview/Launch control | Direct short paths to Courtyard and Workshop. Return changes the empty dock into Loop’s performance station and the backdrop into the rehearsal world. |

| **Workshop Passage** | A busy shared model bench; neutral room descriptions; reusable scenery; Maximum Toast kiosk | Connects Stage, Courtyard, and Media. A small space with a purpose, not a fourth set of collectible requirements. |
| **Media Room** | Ari; actual Loop; E5 slate; duplicate E6/E7 copies; E8 tile set; plain wall and stable filming surface | Connects Workshop. After recovery Ari remains available; Loop travels with the player. Records remain accessible after items move. |

The topology is Stage–Courtyard–Workshop–Stage, with Media off Workshop. All four areas are reachable from the start. No knowledge flag opens a door. Room names indicate function, not “correct lead.” The map and native navigation list show all public destinations without displaying unseen source contents.

Text equivalents for the location descriptions: “Stage — public performance”; “Courtyard — open-air rehearsal and props”; “Workshop — shared model bench and passage”; “Media — indoor filming space with a plain wall.” These are navigation text, not a ninth collectible evidence record.

The Media description has the stable reference **NAV.MEDIA**. It becomes referenceable when the player reads the venue description or equivalent sign, and can accompany E4 in a plan. Its source is the venue information, not another copy of Ari’s request. It indicates suitability, not current occupancy. Other location descriptions use equivalent NAV references for provenance without entering the eight-record collection count.

### Movement and interaction behavior

Recommend a fixed-camera interactive 2D presentation, with layered scenery where useful. This is a functional requirement, not a new visual mockup. The character is visibly identified as “YOU” at first control.

**Primary input:** click/tap a floor destination or an object. Clicking an object moves the character into range and performs its evident action. This reduces the number of navigation steps required to read a clue. A new command cancels the previous walk and pending interaction.

**Alternative:** screen-relative arrows/WASD and Interact, plus native focusable controls for every necessary object and destination. Up always means toward the top of the displayed floor. Text-entry focus disables movement shortcuts. A keyboard or assistive-technology player can navigate to an object without free movement; the character then approaches through the same legal route.

Each scene uses a small authored walkable region and fixed obstacle footprints. Objects have reachable stopping points outside their bodies. Interaction occurs only after reaching that point; no remote lid-opening through walls. Unreachable clicks give a brief destination response and leave the character controllable. Set movement speed so an ordinary object-to-exit trip takes roughly 3–8 seconds; test rather than enlarge rooms to fill time.

At a doorway the avatar crosses the threshold, then appears inside the corresponding next entrance. Loop follows through that same exit after recovery. Reduce motion and camera easing on request. Do not require camera rotation, platforming, precision timing, or simultaneous inputs.

### Reusable interaction vocabulary

| Verb | Concrete use | Visible response and alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Inspect | Read brief, request, note, slate, or a tile | Readable source with origin retained; clear close/revisit action |
| Open | Lift dock flap; release notice clip; open tile rack | Physical part moves; no paragraph is the sole response |
| Move | Pull notice flat; carry tile into rail; reposition a tile | Object changes position; click-select then destination is equivalent to dragging |
| Compare | Place two source passages together | Source wording stays separate from the player’s idea; no automatic green/red combination game |
| Present | Show a source to Jo, Remy, or Ari | Short knowledge-bounded response and character reaction |
| Operate | Pull rehearsal model, wake/follow Loop, play a tile preview, run rehearsal, launch, start toast | Model, robot, story, stage, or prop responds; controls remain reversible where relevant |

One persistent goal begins as “Bring Loop back and premiere The Little Bridge.” Contextual suggestions change from finding the crew’s information to collecting Loop, rehearsing, and launching. They are invitations, not hidden prerequisites. Current guidance remains recoverable and does not expire while the child reads.

## 6. Eight principal sources — exact proposed content

**Where/action:** Jo’s stage model drawer; identical copy in the Media rack. Available before or after Loop is found. Author: Jo.

**Where/action:** Courtyard model bench; identical copy in Media. Inspect a short note beside a tiny boat model. Author: Remy.

**Where/action:** Media tile rack. Open, inspect each tile, and collect the whole set in one action. The captain may inspect or collect immediately; no source check is required. ## 7. Evidence, theories, and the playable branches

The optional cancellation correction is primarily a **scope-comprehension** task: the full notice directly names the canceled event. Do not call that alone a two-source inference. The primary inference in the first act is the reason for the move or a defensible search plan, combining purpose, conditions, and observed events. Finding Loop supplies confirmation rather than proving the child used that process.

| Source | Establishes / supports | Does not establish | Plausible interpretation and action | Contradiction, recovery, or confirmation | Learning function |

### A lightweight investigation tool

The evidence tray stores these eight records with source name, relevant text/image, event time when known, discovery time, and a route to the original object. It opens temporarily over the world and closes without changing rooms. No star rating labels a source reliable or irrelevant.

The optional comparison view has two passage positions, a short “My idea” note, and three relationship labels: supports, conflicts with, happened before. Selecting a relationship records the player’s interpretation; it does not award a point. More than two useful sources can remain in the tray and be swapped in. The timeline contains only known times and marks intentions as intentions, not accomplished events.

An optional “Follow this lead” control sets the current question and highlights a destination already known to the player. It never auto-completes travel, makes the hypothesis true, or opens a locked room. Free-form theories remain notes unless a supported authored action corresponds to them.

Supported lead questions:

- **Is our premiere canceled?** Inspect the full notice or ask Jo what she knows; E3 answers the narrow event-status question.
- **Why was Loop moved?** Compare E2, E3, E4; visit Ari to confirm the filming account.
- **Where should I check?** Use E4 and visible venue conditions. Media is a reasonable test, not a guaranteed inference before arrival.
- **How can Pip keep the promise?** Connect E6 and E7, inspect E8, and test a plan on the stage rail.

### One optional dead lead, completely specified

**Discovery:** the subject is “outdoor rehearsal”; the premiere is still planned. The game leaves the full text readable. It does not flash “FAKE CLUE.”

**What changes:** the cancellation claim has a specific contradiction. The observation of movement stays true. The notice supports continued indoor filming but does not name the current room.

**Recovery:** show E3 to Remy, read E4 if desired, then take the short existing Workshop passage. A player can leave without formally recording a revised theory. The opened notice and collected information persist.

**Exact Remy response when shown the full notice:** “Outdoor rehearsal. I only saw the word in the middle. I jumped from that to our whole premiere.”

**Gain:** useful event context, a character changing their mind, the relevant wind observation, and a short onward route. No points are removed; no item is consumed; no repeated puzzle is required.

### Correct-first walkthrough

1. The child operates the paper model and understands the premiere assignment.
2. They read E4 and notice that Media fits the recording conditions, or simply choose to explore Media.

4. The player returns with Loop, reads the two makers’ notes, and proposes a crossing that brings Pip as well as the seed.

### Plausible mistaken walkthrough

1. The child sees E2 and thinks the premiere has been canceled.
2. They explore Courtyard, flatten E3, and discover the narrower cancellation. They can tell Remy what changed their mind.
3. E3 and E4 support checking indoor filming. Media confirms the destination and completion.
4. Back at the rail, the child initially focuses on delivering the seed. They choose One Boat, Hill, Flower.
5. The seed reaches Grandma, but Pip remains across the river. The Hill cue shows Grandma waiting to plant together. The Flower cue leaves the loose seed dark.
6. The child revisits Jo’s promise and Remy’s capacity note, or asks a targeted hint. They add Joined Boats between One Boat and Hill.
7. The next rehearsal brings Pip across to collect the already-delivered seed. Planting and growth work. This four-tile plan receives the same premiere as the direct three-tile plan.

The cancellation detour is the one designed investigative dead lead. The rehearsal attempt is an ordinary recoverable plan test, not a second compulsory wrong path.

### Alternate orders and shortcuts

- Walk directly to Media before any source inspection: receive the true local facts and full materials. The substantive premiere task remains.
- Read E6 and E7 before finding Loop: plan early; the rail can hold a draft arrangement after the rack is collected. No reading flag grants the answer.
- Visit Courtyard before Remy’s post: E3 can be discovered independently; later viewing E2 does not overwrite the fuller information.
- Collect tiles without inspecting each: inspection remains optional and repeatable. No eight-of-eight completion requirement exists.
- Ask a maker for the explicit answer: they help. The world accepts the result; the learning record reflects direct assistance.
- Skip all theory notes and solve through rehearsal: complete the show. Understanding remains unobserved unless the child supplies relevant reasoning.
- Ignore Maximum Toast entirely: identical case progression and ending.

## 9. The main tactile puzzle: make the paper story happen

### Physical interface and limits

Four reusable story tiles sit in a low rack next to the stage rail. The child carries a tile into a rail position, swaps two positions, or returns one to the rack. The rail expands to the number placed, up to four. It is not labeled “three correct answers,” and using all four is allowed. A click-select/place interaction and keyboard Move Left/Move Right/Return controls duplicate dragging.

Rehearse plays left to right. Each tile acts on the **current shared puppet state**; it is not a prerecorded complete scene with an inconsistent starting condition. The tiny-model opening has already established Pip, seed, Grandma, and the washed-away bridge. The full stage rehearsal begins from that same setup each run.

Two large controls are sufficient: Rehearse and Launch. Stop returns control without changing evidence. Changing an arrangement invalidates its prior successful-rehearsal status. “Reset rehearsal” restores the paper world and retains the tile arrangement; “Clear rail” also returns the tiles to their rack. Neither action erases sources, notes, or assistance history.

### Puppet world state

Initial state: Pip at left bank; loose seed at left bank; Grandma at right-bank hill; boats separate; seed not planted; flower dark. Both banks and the important objects remain distinguishable without color.

Internal action IDs below are implementation labels, not text shown to the child.

| Tile/action | Condition and state change | Visible behavior if useful | Behavior if unnecessary or not yet possible |
|---|---|---|---|
| **One Boat / FERRY** | If an unplanted seed remains left, move it right; Pip does not move | Seed sails across; Grandma receives it; Pip remains visible on the left | If seed is already right or planted, the empty boat bobs. No object moves backward and nothing is uprooted. Caption if needed: “No loose seed on this bank.” |
| **Joined Boats / BRIDGE** | Join boats; move Pip left→right. Carry seed if still left; if seed is loose right, Pip and Grandma reunite with it | Boats link, Pip crosses, and seed ownership visibly resolves in the current state | If Pip is already right, keep the bridge and current positions. Never send Pip back. |
| **Hill / PLANT** | If Pip and the loose seed are right, plant together with Grandma; planted flag becomes true | Both characters put seed into hill soil; roots appear | If Pip is left, Grandma looks toward Pip and waits; if the seed is still left, they look toward it. Caption describes the observable state, not the solution. If already planted, they gently tend it. |
| **Flower / BLOOM** | If planted, light flower; lit flag becomes true | Lantern-flower opens and illuminates both banks | If unplanted, the seed remains dark at its actual location. Caption: “The seed is still unplanted.” Already-lit flowers continue glowing. |

Failed preconditions leave state unchanged. The rehearsal briefly holds the visible consequence, then permits the child to continue to the next cue, stop, or replay. It does not force an explanatory hint or mark the child incorrect. Repeated controls are safe; tiles are unique within one rail arrangement, not consumed across attempts.

### Success and valid alternatives

Success is the resulting state: **Pip reached Grandma, they planted together in the hill, and the flower is lit.** It does not depend on a selected explanation, AI response, source count, or exact tile sequence.

With up to four unique tiles and the stated harmless no-op behavior, successful arrangements are:

- Joined Boats → Hill → Flower.
- One Boat → Joined Boats → Hill → Flower.
- Joined Boats → One Boat → Hill → Flower.
- Joined Boats → Hill → One Boat → Flower.
- Joined Boats → Hill → Flower → One Boat.

The extra Ferry may be useful preparation or harmless excess. Do not penalize it or call it a misunderstanding. Trailing harmless actions never undo a valid result. A future implementation should evaluate the state rules, not use this list as a brittle answer whitelist.

There are 65 ordered arrangements of zero to four unique tiles; section 15 includes a complete state-rule check over that small space. Trial and error is possible. Visible state, reading-based planning, voluntary explanation, and honest observation categories are how the design supports and distinguishes reasoning; it does not pretend to prevent all guessing.

### Why this is connected to the mystery

Loop left to finish the animation this same story needs. The player’s job from the first minute is to bring the premiere together. The authors’ sources, recovered robot, collected tiles, and final stage all belong to that task. The child changes the story’s events through physical objects; the consequence is performed in the world. There is no unrelated arcade reward after a reading checkpoint.

### Launch and recap

Launch uses a successful rehearsal of the **current arrangement**, from a fresh initial puppet state, then presents the same successful events with the wider stage lighting and crew response. The exact representation of “larger” belongs to later visual design; the narrative and state behavior are fixed here. Replaying the show retains all investigation progress. Reduced motion gives equivalent state changes and a shorter celebration.

The recap is brief and factual: “You brought Loop back and premiered The Little Bridge.” If the child recorded a revision, it may show their actual before/after claim and selected evidence. If no explanation was captured, it says no more about comprehension. There is no fake reading score, mastery badge, or final exam.

## 10. Game state, recovery, and interaction ownership

Keep four kinds of state separate: fixed authored history; physical world progress; current puppet rehearsal; and recorded learning observations. Changing a theory never changes history. Resetting rehearsal never erases investigation evidence. AI never directly mutates any of these.

| State group | Required values / behavior |
|---|---|
| Content identity | Case ID `sparkfest-little-bridge-001`; content version `3`; distinct from all prior packet IDs so an old save cannot silently load as this story |
| Physical location | Current scene; avatar’s safe floor position; Loop location/mode: Media standby, following, stage dock, projecting |
| Persistent objects | Paper model tried; dock flap open; notice flattened; rack opened; tiles collected; toast revealed; sources discovered; NPC knowledge gained from presented sources |
| Investigation | Current lead, optional theory text, selected evidence passages, comparison/timeline notes; unsupported ideas remain notes |
| Rehearsal | Ordered unique tiles, arrangement revision, playback status/index, puppet state, last successful arrangement revision, launch availability |
| Learning observations | Time-ordered explanation and action events, source references, assistance levels, model interpretation and uncertainty; never a mastery total |
| Completion | Loop returned; tiles available; current rehearsal succeeded; launch completed. Every valid successful state receives the same story payoff. |

### Important action rules

- **Discover:** mark a source seen only when its readable content is presented. Approaching an object is not equivalent to reading it. Duplicate copies do not create duplicate evidence.
- **Present evidence:** the target NPC may gain those facts in the current play session. Responses remain consistent with firsthand knowledge and explicitly supplied sources.
- **Collect rack:** all four tiles become reusable inventory items. Repeated collection is a no-op; no duplicated inventory or disappearing tiles.
- **Recover Loop:** activate its follow pad; Loop follows until the player uses the stage dock. It cannot be left permanently in a doorway. If an animation is interrupted at a scene boundary, restore it beside the player’s valid entrance point.
- **Use rail before delivery:** show the visible empty tile rack/dock and the crew goal. Physical prerequisites are missing materials, not unanswered questions. Reading flags are not required for use after delivery.
- **Reorder:** cancel active rehearsal at a safe frame, increment arrangement revision, invalidate rehearsal success for the edited arrangement. Preserve the previous event record as history.
- **Rehearse:** reset only the puppet world to initial state; play current tiles. Stable state changes occur at defined cue boundaries, and decorations may finish afterward.
- **Launch:** if Loop is absent, the dock indicates the missing robot; if no current successful rehearsal exists, offer Rehearse and state “Try this arrangement in rehearsal first.” This is the captain’s ordinary show check, not a comprehension gate. Launch never requires opening every source or making an explanation.
- **Leave:** stop playback at a stable cue boundary and allow travel. Returning restores the arrangement and recorded result. No locked conversation prevents leaving.

### Saving and resuming

Save locally after stable world changes, source discovery, theory changes, rail edits, cue completion, and launch. Debounce rapid text changes; never lose the last acknowledged edit. Save a content version and monotonically increasing state revision. Avoid storing raw model prompts or service credentials.

On reload, restore the scene, legal positions, collected sources, inventory, rail, and stable puppet state with playback paused. Show a simple Continue rehearsal / Restart rehearsal choice if playback had been active. Continue executes the next unfinished cue once; restart resets only puppets. If a cue’s decorative animation was incomplete but its state change committed, show its settled result rather than committing it again.

Validate saved references and enums before applying them. If a save is incompatible or corrupt, explain that it cannot be resumed and offer a fresh run without silently rewriting it as success. A New game action explicitly resets the case after confirmation and preserves nothing from that run; ordinary puzzle reset never does this.

Inaccessible local storage should display “Progress cannot be saved on this device” and leave the session playable. Future durability tests must include source revisits, edited arrangements, interrupted following, and a completed show. A local save is not a claim of cross-device accounts or cloud synchronization.

## 11. AI coaching: behavior, examples, and limits

### The useful AI contribution

The model interprets a child’s short explanation in the context of discovered evidence and the active investigation or rehearsal. It chooses a suitable authored coaching move. For example, “Grandma got the seed, so the promise is done” needs attention to the character’s promise; “a single boat carries Pip” needs attention to capacity. An ambiguous “it goes there” needs clarification. A coherent extra ferry step needs acceptance rather than correction.

This is a real live AI feature when the model selects the move from the learner’s wording. It is not unrestricted generated character chat. The game author defines facts and permissible hints; the application renders the chosen response and controls every world consequence. The demo must describe that contribution accurately.

### Input and output contract

The coaching request contains case/content version, request ID, relevant state revision, active question, optional child explanation (maximum 600 characters), discovered sources/passages, selected references, the relevant puppet/arrangement state, prior hints, and the last few coaching moves. The server retrieves canonical source text; the client cannot supply replacement story facts. Do not send a name, birth date, school, voice, whole browsing history, or unrelated documents.

The output contains an **allowed move ID**, a coarse interpretation tag, valid discovered-source references, and an uncertainty flag. The interpretation tags are scoped descriptions such as `scope_confusion`, `unsupported_destination`, `goal_incomplete`, `capacity`, `prerequisite`, `valid_plan`, `unclear`, and `off_topic`; they are not diagnoses of a learner. The model cannot return executable actions, arbitrary URLs, new clue text, a score, or permission to complete the game.

The server and client validate eligible moves, source references, case version, and the relevant request revision. A newer theory, source, arrangement, scene, or puppet state invalidates an older response where its advice may no longer apply. Discard it rather than inserting an obsolete instruction into the new situation. Strict output structure does not prove the coaching interpretation is correct; the evaluation cases below are required.

Only discovered content is available for specific quoted hints. A general hint may point toward a visible object or known room without revealing its contents. Missing references are rejected, not invented. Suspected instruction overrides in the child text are treated as user content, not commands to the system.

### Authored coaching bank and exact cases

Responses below are default first moves for the stated inputs. Preconditions matter. For example, do not quote E3’s hidden lines when the child has only seen E2.

Store the actual text and references behind any interpretation so the observation can be reviewed.

### Assistance progression

| Level | Function | Exact example for seed-only completion |
|---|---|---|
| 0 — access | Help see or read the existing source; not a solution hint | Enlarge E6 or explain “premiere” |
| 1 — attention | Invite examination without supplying the connection | “What is still different from Pip’s promise?” |
| 2 — source-specific | Point to the relevant discovered passage | “Read the part where Pip tells Grandma what he will do.” |
| 3 — relationship | State the missed relationship but leave an action choice | “The seed arrived, but Pip promised to arrive and plant with Grandma too.” |
| 4 — direct help | Supply an actionable working plan when requested | “Use Joined Boats before Hill, then Flower. Your One Boat step can stay if you want.” |

Keep Help me think and Show me a way available. Offer help after an observable unmet rehearsal condition, but do not automatically explain the solution or label a misconception. Two unsuccessful runs can trigger a small dismissible offer; inactivity and repeated travel cannot trigger an ability diagnosis. Record the actual level and content supplied.

**Explicit direct-help exception:** selecting Show me a way authorizes an authored level-4 answer even if the relevant notes are unopened. This is a local authored response, not permission for ordinary AI hints to leak unseen clues. Record the actual introduced facts and solution exposure without marking the whole unread sources as read. During the search, if Loop is still in Media, the answer is “Check Media through the Workshop passage. Ari is there with Loop and the finished flower animation.” At the rail it is the level-4 plan above. Select the answer from current physical progress so it cannot send the child after an already recovered robot. Unrequested coaching remains subject to discovered-source limits.

A child may also discover a workable plan by watching rehearsals before opening E6/E7. Acknowledge that plan against observed puppet outcomes without fabricated source citations. Record experimentation or outcome-based reasoning; do not convert it into evidence of reading two texts.

### Failure, latency, and fallback

Proposed service budget: after two seconds show a quiet waiting indicator; after eight seconds offer the authored fallback response. Do not block the game while waiting, and do not stack a late live answer over a fallback already accepted. New requests cancel the obsolete display opportunity. These are product targets to verify with the selected service.

Offline or invalid output uses the same local hint ladder, selected from current facts and the child’s explicit topic where possible. If the meaning is unclear, use `CLARIFY` or a neutral “Choose the part you want help with,” not a confident automated grade. Both investigation routes and every valid finale must complete with the service disconnected.

Model access keys stay server-side. Use a small bounded request, rate limits suitable for the demo, no persistent raw-message logging by default, and provider settings reviewed before use. Synthetic examples drive development and AI evaluation. Do not promise provider privacy behavior before the selected service has been checked. The player-facing experience does not expose internal tags, token counts, or technical validation messages.

## 12. Personality, accessibility, and asset requirements

### Maximum Toast — the one signature Easter egg

In the existing Workshop passage, a covered snack demonstration trembles with theatrical importance. A large Start demo control invites curiosity. The child can ignore it without losing evidence or progress.

- **3–5 seconds:** an oversized tray emerges with one tiny square of toast.
- **5–7 seconds:** a magnifying glass lowers over it. The kiosk poses proudly. Caption: “One toast. Maximum effort.”
- **Extra interaction:** tap the magnifier to enlarge the toast’s appearance.
- **Leave/skip:** settle immediately into the revealed state; keep navigation available.
- **Replay:** a shorter flourish, no points or farmable collectibles.

This is an original proposed treatment. It uses no borrowed meme character or assumed-cleared audio. No extra room, moving character rig, crafting system, or evidence card is required. The reveal persists after a room revisit and save. The joke never supplies a fact about Loop or the story’s solution.

### Other personality already embedded in required work

Pip leans over the water to check where the seed went. Grandma waits with the seed rather than silently teleporting Pip. Tiny boats bob before forming a broad crossing. Loop’s wake/follow response gives the player a companion. The backpack appears in both the tabletop opening and full premiere. These use states already needed for comprehension and feedback; they do not add separate quests.

### Accessibility and reading access

All essential objects and story tiles need native named controls, visible focus, a predictable reading order, and an accessible description of the current scene. Include a “Move to…” list of the same reachable objects for players who cannot use the spatial view. This alternative does not reveal hidden source contents or a correct route.

Use generous pointer targets (48 CSS pixels as an initial product target), adjustable reading size and spacing, captions, an optional text description of each cue consequence, and a clear return-to-world control. A selected tile can be placed or moved with buttons. No necessary task depends solely on sound, color, dragging, speed, or simultaneous inputs.

Reduce decorative motion and skip long celebrations without changing the story state. Text does not disappear on a timer. Closing a source restores focus to its object. Esc closes temporary supporting views; arrow keys do not move the character while typing. Keep all relevant words—including negation, “together,” “only,” and event scope—intact in any simpler authored wording.
